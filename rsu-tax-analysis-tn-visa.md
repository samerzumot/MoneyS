# RSU Sale Tax Implications for TN Visa Holders After Leaving the US

## Executive Summary

**Short Answer:** Generally **NO**, you cannot sell vested RSUs tax-free after moving from the US, even if you move to a country with no capital gains tax. The US will still tax you on the sale, and you may have additional tax obligations depending on your circumstances.

## Key Tax Considerations

### 1. US Tax Obligations on RSUs

#### When RSUs Vest (While in the US)
- **Ordinary Income Tax**: When RSUs vest, the fair market value (FMV) at vesting is treated as **ordinary income** and subject to:
  - Federal income tax (typically 22-37% depending on income)
  - State income tax (if applicable)
  - FICA taxes (Social Security and Medicare) - typically 7.65% for employees
- **Withholding**: Your employer typically withholds shares to cover taxes at vesting

#### When You Sell RSUs (After Leaving the US)
- **Capital Gains Tax**: The difference between the sale price and the FMV at vesting is treated as a **capital gain**
- **US Tax Obligation**: As a non-resident alien (NRA) after leaving the US, you may still owe US taxes on:
  - **US-source income**: Gains from selling US company stock are generally considered US-source income
  - **Withholding**: Brokers may withhold 30% (or lower treaty rate) on capital gains for NRAs

### 2. Tax Status After Leaving the US

#### Non-Resident Alien (NRA) Status
- Once you leave the US and are no longer a tax resident, you become a **Non-Resident Alien (NRA)**
- NRAs are subject to different tax rules:
  - **30% withholding** on US-source income (unless reduced by tax treaty)
  - **Capital gains** on US company stock are generally **taxable** in the US
  - May need to file Form 1040-NR

#### US-Canada Tax Treaty
- The US-Canada tax treaty may provide some relief:
  - **Article XIII(4)**: Capital gains from the sale of shares may be exempt from US tax if:
    - You are a resident of Canada (not the US) at the time of sale
    - The shares are not "immovable property" or shares of a US real estate company
    - However, this is complex and depends on specific circumstances

### 3. Tax Implications in Destination Country

#### Countries with No Capital Gains Tax
Even if your destination country has no capital gains tax:
- **US taxes still apply**: You still owe US taxes on the sale
- **No double taxation relief**: You can't use foreign tax credits if the destination country doesn't tax you
- **Potential double taxation**: You may be taxed in both countries if the destination country also taxes capital gains

#### Example: Moving to a Tax Haven
- If you move to a country with no capital gains tax (e.g., UAE, Singapore for certain residents):
  - **US will still tax you** on the capital gains
  - You may be able to claim the US-Canada tax treaty benefits if you maintain Canadian residency
  - The destination country won't tax you, but the US will

### 4. Important Considerations

#### Timing Matters
- **Selling before leaving**: If you sell while still a US tax resident, you pay:
  - Capital gains tax at your marginal rate (0%, 15%, or 20% for long-term gains)
  - State taxes (if applicable)
- **Selling after leaving**: As an NRA, you may face:
  - 30% withholding (or treaty rate)
  - Potential complications with tax filing
  - Less favorable tax treatment in some cases

#### Residency Status
- **US Tax Residency**: Determined by:
  - **Substantial Presence Test**: 183 days in the US over 3 years
  - **Green Card Test**: Having a green card
- **Canadian Tax Residency**: You may be considered a tax resident of Canada if:
  - You maintain significant ties to Canada
  - You don't establish residency elsewhere
  - You return to Canada after leaving the US

#### Expatriation Tax (Generally Not Applicable)
- **Not applicable to TN visa holders**: The expatriation tax (exit tax) generally applies to:
  - Long-term permanent residents (green card holders for 8+ years)
  - US citizens renouncing citizenship
  - TN visa holders are typically not subject to this

### 5. Practical Scenarios

#### Scenario 1: Sell After Moving to Canada
- **US Tax**: May be exempt under US-Canada tax treaty if you're a Canadian resident
- **Canadian Tax**: 50% of capital gains included in income (marginal rate)
- **Result**: Likely pay Canadian taxes, potentially avoid US taxes

#### Scenario 2: Sell After Moving to Tax Haven (UAE, etc.)
- **US Tax**: Likely 30% withholding or treaty rate (if Canadian resident)
- **Destination Tax**: None
- **Result**: Pay US taxes, no destination country taxes

#### Scenario 3: Sell While Still in US
- **US Tax**: Capital gains at your marginal rate (0-20% for long-term)
- **State Tax**: If applicable
- **Result**: Typically most favorable tax treatment

### 6. Recommendations

1. **Consult a Tax Professional**: This is complex and depends on:
   - Your specific circumstances
   - The exact timing of your move
   - Your residency status in both countries
   - The specific tax treaties involved

2. **Consider Selling Before Leaving**: 
   - May result in lower tax rates
   - Simpler tax filing
   - Avoid NRA withholding complications

3. **Understand Your Residency Status**:
   - Determine if you'll be a Canadian resident, US resident, or resident of another country
   - This affects which tax treaties apply

4. **Plan for Withholding**:
   - If selling as an NRA, expect 30% withholding (or treaty rate)
   - May need to file US tax return to claim refunds

5. **Document Everything**:
   - Keep records of vesting dates and FMV
   - Track your residency status
   - Maintain documentation for tax treaty claims

## Key Takeaways

1. **US taxes generally still apply** when selling RSUs after leaving the US
2. **Moving to a country with no capital gains tax doesn't eliminate US tax obligations**
3. **The US-Canada tax treaty may provide some relief** if you maintain Canadian residency
4. **Timing and residency status are critical** factors in determining tax liability
5. **Professional tax advice is essential** for your specific situation
